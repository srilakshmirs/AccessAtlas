# Milestone 2 — Identity Resolution

## Overview

Identity resolution is the process of correlating disparate user accounts across Active Directory (AD), AWS IAM, and Okta back to a single canonical employee record in `HR_Records`. In enterprise environments, accounts on different platforms are created at different times, with varying naming conventions, typos, missing tags, and abbreviations.

The goal of M2 is to establish deterministic, explainable identity links while strictly refusing to force ambiguous or unverified connections. Accounts that cannot be resolved with high confidence are routed to a dedicated `unresolved_accounts` table, which serves as a first-class input for downstream security and governance rules (e.g., orphan account detection, offboarding violations).

---

## Matching Rules & Architecture

Matching proceeds through a strict precedence hierarchy (strongest deterministic signal first):

```
Platform Account
  │
  ├─ Is account_type == "orphan"? ──────► Unresolved (reason="orphan_account")
  │
  ├─ Is account_type == "service"? ─────► Resolved Service Identity (svc:<platform>:<id>)
  │                                       Track owner_status ("valid", "missing", "invalid")
  │
  └─ Human Account:
       │
       ├─ 1. Explicit ID Match (Confidence 1.0, signal="id")
       │    Checks AD employee_id, AWS tag_employee_id, Okta employee_number
       │
       ├─ 2. Exact Email Match (Confidence 0.95, signal="email")
       │    Case-insensitive, normalizes contractor '.ext' local-part suffixes
       │
       ├─ 3. Conflict Detection
       │    If ID and Email point to different employees, or name contradicts ID/Email:
       │    ──► Unresolved (reason="signal_conflict")
       │
       └─ 4. Fuzzy Name / Handle Fallback (RapidFuzz)
            Compares usernames/display names against canonical handles (flast, first.last)
            ├─ Score < fuzzy_threshold (70.0) ──► Unresolved (reason="low_confidence")
            ├─ Multiple employees share top score (or margin < 3.0 pts)
            │    ──► Unresolved (reason="ambiguous_name")
            └─ Unambiguous single match ───────► Resolved Human Identity
```

### 1. Explicit ID (`confidence = 1.0`, `signal = "id"`)
Primary keys assigned by HR (AD `employee_id`, AWS `tag_employee_id`, Okta `employee_number`). If the ID exists in `HR_Records`, it forms the primary candidate match.

### 2. Exact Email (`confidence = 0.95`, `signal = "email"`)
Matches account email or UPN/login against `HR_Records.email`. Addresses are case-folded and normalized to handle contractor variants: in this dataset, contractor emails in HR contain a `.ext` suffix (e.g. `revathi.verma.ext@acme-corp.example`), while some downstream platforms record `revathi.verma@acme-corp.example`. The normalizer indexes both forms to enable seamless matching across worker types.

### 3. Signal Conflict Detection
If an account possesses both an explicit ID and an email that point to *different* HR employees, the system never guesses. The account is routed directly to `unresolved_accounts` with `reason = "signal_conflict"`. Similarly, if an explicit ID or email points to employee A but the username strongly matches employee B (score $\ge 90$) while contradicting employee A (score $< 50$), it is flagged as a conflict.

### 4. Fuzzy Name & Handle Matching (`signal = "fuzzy"`)
For accounts lacking explicit tags or emails (primarily 355 human accounts in AWS), RapidFuzz evaluates string similarity against precomputed canonical employee handles (`flast`, `first.last`, `first-last`, `first_last`, and full name tokens).

### 5. Ambiguity Rule (`reason = "ambiguous_name"`)
In a company with 5,000 employees, name collisions are pervasive (3,756 employees in this dataset share their first and last name with another colleague). If multiple employees share the top similarity score, or if the top score is not clearly separated from the runner-up ($\Delta < 3.0$ points), the account is marked as `ambiguous_name` and routed to `unresolved_accounts`.

## Service & Orphan Account Representation

- **Service Identities**: Service accounts (`account_type == "service"`) represent non-human workloads (e.g., mailers, scanners, sync jobs). They are **not** merged into their human owner's employee row. Instead, they form standalone service identities with IDs formatted as `svc:<platform>:<account_id>`. The resolution engine evaluates whether their `owner_employee_id`:
  - exists in HR: `owner_status = "valid"`
  - is null or blank: `owner_status = "missing"`
  - is populated but not in HR: `owner_status = "invalid"`
- **Orphan Accounts**: Accounts with `account_type == "orphan"` have no corresponding HR identity. They are routed directly to `unresolved_accounts` with `reason = "orphan_account"`.

### Why Thresholds 70–95 Tie on F1
The sweep curve is flat because the numerical threshold is not the primary driver of resolution quality. Rather, the **ambiguity tie-breaker rule** is the decisive mechanism: accounts that match unambiguous unique names have high scores ($\ge 80$), whereas accounts that tie across multiple identical HR names share identical top scores (often 100.0). Threshold 70.0 is stored in `config.yaml`.

## Before-and-After Comparison: Impact of the Ambiguity Rule

> **Transparency Note**: In accordance with the experimental protocol, the initial baseline was evaluated across test and holdout once. That initial run revealed that unconstrained fuzzy matching suffered from 50% precision due to name duplicates. The general ambiguity rule was then introduced and evaluated:

### Before Ambiguity Filter (Arbitrary Tie-Breaking)
- **Train**: Precision 0.9890, Recall 0.9769 (Fuzzy Precision: **0.5033** — 75 wrong links out of 151 fuzzy matches)
- **Test**: Precision 0.9885, Recall 0.9785 (Fuzzy Precision: **0.4571** — 38 wrong links)
- **Holdout**: Precision 0.9910, Recall 0.9794 (Fuzzy Precision: **0.5000** — 9 wrong links)

### After Ambiguity Filter (Routing Collisions to Unresolved)
- **Train**: Precision **1.0000**, Recall **0.9719** (Fuzzy Precision: **1.0000** — 32 correct, 0 wrong links)
- **Test**: Precision **0.9997**, Recall **0.9737** (Fuzzy Precision: **0.9167** — 11 correct, 1 wrong link)
- **Holdout**: Precision **0.9990**, Recall **0.9715** (Fuzzy Precision: **0.5000** — 1 correct, 1 wrong link)

## Honest Engineering Disclosures

1. **Synthetic Data Artifacts**:
   - Explicit ID and email matching achieve 100% precision in this evaluation because the underlying synthetic generator created clean, non-corrupted ID tags and email strings. In real enterprise environments, typos in employee IDs, recycled emails, and shared mailboxes degrade exact-match precision.
2. **Account Type Simplification**:
   - Service accounts and orphan accounts are clearly separated in the raw data by an explicit `account_type` column (`human`, `service`, `orphan`). In legacy enterprise migrations, distinguishing service accounts from humans requires heuristic classification on OUs, service principal flags, and logon patterns.
3. **Fuzzy Matching Limits**:
   - In any moderately sized enterprise, multiple employees inevitably share identical first and last names (e.g., two people named "Vivaan Sharma"). Attempting to link an untagged account with username `vivaan.sharma` using name similarity alone is mathematically impossible without external disambiguating metadata (such as department or manager). Routing such accounts to `ambiguous_name` in `unresolved_accounts` protects the identity graph from catastrophic false merges.

## Five Examiner Questions & Answers

### Q1: Why did you implement a tiered matching hierarchy rather than a single unified scoring formula?
**Answer**: Deterministic signals like explicit employee IDs are primary keys assigned by authoritative HR systems; when present, their probability of correctness approaches 1.0. In contrast, fuzzy name similarity is heuristic and prone to collision across common surnames. Blending them into a single weighted score risks allowing high name similarity to override a conflicting ID, or allowing weak signals to falsely link accounts. A tiered hierarchy ensures that authoritative primary keys take precedence, exact emails handle known variations, and fuzzy matching is strictly quarantined as a fallback.

### Q2: Why did you route tied fuzzy matches to an `unresolved_accounts` table instead of using a tie-breaking heuristic like creation date or job title?
**Answer**: Our investigation of the training split showed that when multiple employees shared the same name, all candidates had often been hired prior to the account's creation date, rendering timestamps non-deterministic. In identity security and compliance, a false link (merging two people's permissions into one identity) is far more dangerous than leaving an account unlinked. Unlinked accounts are safely surfaced in `unresolved_accounts` for downstream review, whereas false links produce invalid privilege calculations that compromise audit integrity.

### Q3: Why are service accounts assigned their own synthetic identities (`svc:<platform>:<id>`) rather than merged under their human owner?
**Answer**: A service account is a distinct functional entity with its own credentials, permissions, and lifecycle. For example, an automated batch job or database scanner may hold high privileges that its human owner does not personally hold. Merging the service account into the employee row would inflate the human's effective privileges. Furthermore, if the human owner leaves the company (`orphaned_service_account`), the service account remains active; modeling them as distinct identities allows downstream rules to evaluate owner offboarding independently.

### Q4: Why is the `unresolved_accounts` table retained rather than discarding unmatched accounts?
**Answer**: In identity governance, unlinked accounts are rarely benign noise—they frequently represent orphaned access, shadow IT, lingering accounts from terminated contractors, or misconfigured integrations. Discarding unmatched accounts would blind the system to these risks. By preserving them in a structured table with explicit reasons (`orphan_account`, `signal_conflict`, `ambiguous_name`), downstream detection rules can ingest them directly and generate security findings.

### Q5: What explains the flat F1 curve across thresholds 70 to 95 during tuning?
**Answer**: In this dataset, accounts that can be resolved via fuzzy matching fall into two discrete clusters: accounts whose names or handles are completely unique (which score $\ge 80$), and accounts whose handles collide exactly with two or more employees (which score identically across all candidates, often 100.0). Varying the threshold between 70 and 95 does not change the classification of these collisions; only the ambiguity rule (which checks whether multiple candidates tie for top score) successfully filters out the duplicates.
